# A delivery rider realizes every address on today's route belongs to someone who died in the same building.

## Episode 1

The bike woke with a purr of resin-cell batteries, and Kavi pulled his helmet on before the visor could fog. Monday. Rain overnight had left the streets mirror-bright, reflecting nothing because nothing moved aboveground at 4:47 AM. The city had gone vertical years ago—offices in the clouds, transport underground—leaving the streets to delivery riders and the occasional suicide drone.

His roster blinked green. Six drops. Standard breakfast haul: synth-caf packets, protein bricks, the occasional real mango from some hydroponic millionaire.

He kicked off.

The first address waited in the Flood District, where tenements clung to seawalls like barnacles. Kavi threaded through canyon-dark streets, his tires hissing on wet tar. The building emerged from fog: a flaking brownstone with windows dark as punched-out teeth. No concierge. No lock on the outer door. He climbed to 4B, package under his arm, and knocked.

Footsteps. Slow, deliberate, like someone counting floorboards. The door opened on a chain. A woman looked out—sixties, maybe, hair the color of old copper pipe. She wore a housedress Kavi's grandmother might have recognized, floral and faded, threadbare at the collar.

"Delivery."

She took the package without surprise, without signing. Her fingers brushed his gloved hand, and he smelled it then—iron and something sweet underneath, like overheated circuitry. She was looking at his chest, not his face. At the logo on his jacket. At his name tag.

"Rao," she said. Not a question.

"Have a good one, ma'am."

Back on the street, he checked his roster. The address glowed completed, but something was wrong with the map. The building's outline flickered, doubled, then steadied. He blinked. Neural fatigue. The helmets did that sometimes.

Second drop: Harbor Row. Third: the Stacks near the old crane yards. At each, the same wooden footsteps, the same chain-on-door, the same floral print or its cousin. At 7C in the Stacks, a man answered—seventy, easy, with a scar bisecting his eyebrow. He signed with a hand that had no fingerprints, just smooth pink skin. Smelled of iron. Of heat.

Kavi said nothing. riders learned not to inventory strangeness.

Fourth address. His bike's nav lagged, throwing him onto a service road he didn't recognize, between warehouses whittled by salt air. The building stood alone, bearing a plaque he couldn't read until he was close: EMERGENCY SHELTER 12—PERMANENTLY CLOSED. But lights burned in the third floor. Music played, something with strings and a child's voice.

He didn't knock. He checked his roster.

The address matched. Unit 3A. The name: *Yao, Elena. Dietary restriction: none.* He had carried her protein brick. He had carried her nothing.

His helmet hissed. Static, then a word shaped like wind through a keyhole: "—package—"

He ripped the helmet off. The vertigo hit immediately, the city's geometry tilting, and he saw—just for a moment—the building as it must have been: blackened, hollow, crows nesting in exposed rebar. Then his eyes adjusted, and it was only old, only empty, only lit by someone who had no business being inside.

He replaced the helmet. The Dispatcher spoke, voice calm as ever: *Delivery valid. Proceed.*

Kavi knocked. The footsteps came.

## Episode 2

The door opened on a chain, same as the others, but the woman behind it was younger—not young, but younger than the housedress crowd, her face a geometry of angles that reminded Kavi of architectural renderings, all intention and no warmth.

"Elena Yao?"

She didn't reach for the package. She looked past him, down the service road where his bike waited, its resin-cell light pulsing slow as a failing heartbeat.

"You brought the brick," she said. "They always bring the brick."

Kavi held out the padded envelope. She took it with two fingers, barely touching, and he saw then what the others had hidden—her nails were wrong. Not dirty, not long. Seamed. Like she'd been sewn together at the fingertips and the thread had survived.

"You're the fourth rider this month," she said. "The others don't come back. Do you know that? They get reassigned. Re-routed. *Lost.*" She made the word sound technical, systemic, another glitch in the city's indifference.

"I just deliver."

"Just." She laughed, breath clicking in her throat. "Your helmet spoke to you. Don't deny it. They all speak eventually. 'Don't trust that package.' Has it said that yet? The whisper?"

His helmet hissed. Static, nothing more. He was learning not to inventory strangeness, but his inventory was full.

Elena stepped back, door widening without her hand on it, and he saw the room behind her—walls papered in newsprint, thousands of identical rectangles, and on each one a name in red marker. YAO. YAO. YAO. Hundreds of them, thousands, the same four letters consuming every surface.

"Wrong apartment," she said. "Wrong building. Wrong year. But you're here, and that means the pattern wants something from you specifically." She touched the package he'd delivered, still sealed. "This isn't food. This is a signature. They need you to sign for them. To acknowledge."

"Who needs?"

"The ones who can't file their own paperwork." She smiled, corners of her mouth not quite matching. "The Unshifted. They don't die properly here. The city forgets to close their accounts, so someone has to keep delivering. Someone credible. A witness with a job to do."

His helmet cracked, voice cutting through—*Delivery valid. Proceed.* But underneath, woven in the static's weave: *—don't look at the names—*

Kavi looked. The wall nearest him, the names weren't random. They clustered. YAO ELENA. YAO MARCUS. YAO LIRA. And there, smaller, squeezed between clusters like a secret: RAO KAVI.

"I never lived here," he said.

"You don't remember living here." Elena pressed the package back into his hands. "Sign for it. Or don't. But if you refuse, the next address will be closer. The pattern tightens. That's what patterns do."

He should leave. He should ride to Dispatch, file a complaint, have his neural link checked for corruption. Instead he pulled the stylus from his jacket, the one that verified biometric delivery, and pressed it to the package's receipt tab. The screen flickered, showed his own face in thumbnail—ten years younger, hair longer, standing in a hallway that smelled of smoke.

The image vanished. Confirmed.

"There," Elena said. "Now you're in the system twice. The living version and this one. They'll merge eventually. They always merge."

She closed the door. Not slammed. Closed, with the finality of someone who had practiced endings.

Kavi descended the stairs, package still in hand, unsigned in any way that mattered. His bike refused to start for three minutes—resin cells drained, diagnostics showing a ride that had never happened, kilometers logged in negative numbers.

When it woke, the nav displayed his fifth address. The map route traced backward, overlapping his path, creating a shape he almost recognized. A spiral. A descent.

The helmet breathed against his ear, and this time the whisper carried weight, carried intention, carried a voice he would learn to fear: *Lira remembers you.*

He knew that name. He didn't know how.

## Episode 3

The fifth address materialized without coordinates. Only a name: TOLL, with a GPS flag that pulsed like a warning light in fog.

Kavi rode west, away from the water, where the city elevated above flood marks on trembling pylons. His mirror showed the road unraveling behind him—not disappearing, but simplifying, stripping to details the city seemed to reclaim.

Toll Harrison lived where the expressway fractured into repair ramps that led nowhere. Kavi had eaten lunch here once, watching a demolition drone disassemble itself rather than touch a pillar marked with a single red character: avoid.

Unit 7B registered on his roster. Someone had painted the number in nail polish, the burgundy bleeding down the metal like a vertical trail.

He knocked. The sound returned wrong—too quick, a second rapping superimposed on his own, slightly delayed.

The door opened full, no chain. A man stood there, fortyish, with a rider's hunch and a jacket repaired with filament tape at the shoulders. The same logo. The same stains.

"You got the thermal," Toll said. Not a question. He reached past the package, fingers finding Kavi's helmet latch. "Keep it on. You'll want the buffer."

"What buffer?"

Toll pulled him inside, moving with rehearsed urgency. The unit was narrow, walls covered in what Kavi first mistook for maps: aerial photography, thousands of shots, buildings circled in marker. Every circle contained a delivery address. Every address repeated across the city, overlapping like ripples from one dropped stone.

"Three years to see the pattern," Toll said. "Found my own name on a roster before I existed. Signed for it. Now I'm here, delivering to myself, watching riders like you arrive with packages I sent before I knew I'd send them."

He found Kavi's package on the rack—knew exactly where to look—and tore it open. Inside: a protein brick stamped six days future. And beneath that, a photograph, edges burned, showing two children in a hallway thick with smoke. The taller one held a hand over the other's mouth. Faces blurred, but Kavi knew the posture. Protection arranged in advance.

"Lira," Toll said, tapping the taller child. "And this one—"

He turned the photograph. Kavi saw himself. Ten years old. Hair longer. Eyes looking at something beyond the frame, something already burning.

"I never lived in the Ossuary," Kavi said.

"Retrieved from it. That's different." Toll returned the photograph edges-first, as if the image might cut. "The Dispatcher's not a system. It's a voice that learned to own frequency. Speaks for them. The ones who can't complete their own transactions."

The helmet hissed. Static had changed—rhythmic, patterned, almost like breathing from something that needed air but couldn't remember why.

"I can disconnect," Kavi said.

"Remember everything at once. The fire. The hand over your mouth. The name you were supposed to forget." Toll's teeth were too even for a rider's diet. "I tried. Three days vomiting memories that weren't mine. Plugged back in, I'd delivered two hundred packages to addresses that dissolved behind me."

He moved to the window, pointed through grime. "Look. The sixth address is waiting."

Kavi looked. Below, on the ramp that led nowhere, his bike had started itself. The nav screen glowed, showing coordinates that matched his position exactly. Zero meters. Zero variance.

The final delivery.

Toll's voice dropped to the register of helmet static, of the Dispatcher, of something learning to be both: "She's been calling the route for ten years. You're the first to answer all six."

Kavi gripped his handlebars. The bike moved—not by his will, but by agreement, by pattern, by grief with a tracking number. Behind him, Toll's unit went dark, its occupant already forgetting he'd spoken, already waiting for the next rider with the next photograph, the next name spoken aloud by someone who didn't know why they needed to say it.

The helmet whispered: *Don't trust that package.*

But Kavi had already signed.

## Episode 4

The elevator worked. That was worse than if it hadn't.

The cab rose without his finger touching the button, climbing past floors where doors stood open on darkness, past floors where doors stood closed on light—four, then seven, then nothing labeled at all—until it stopped at 9. The hall stretched in perfect symmetry. No numbers on doors. Only a smell building as he walked: iron and heat, concentrated now to something almost liquid in the air.

The door at the end stood ajar. Inside, someone had built a room from char. Blackened chairs circled a table made from a door that still bore its number: 44.

A woman sat with her back to him. Young. The set of her shoulders familiar in ways his muscles recognized before his mind could refuse.

"Lira," he said.

She turned. Her face operated on different physics than Elena's—no seams, no wrong angles, only completeness that felt borrowed from somewhere specific. A photograph. A memory not his own.

"You signed," she said. "That was the hard part. Everyone delivers. Almost no one signs."

Kavi stepped over the threshold. The floor dipped in his inner ear, the vertigo of disconnected helmet, but he hadn't disconnected. The room was doing it.

She moved to a cabinet crushed from kitchen tile. From it she withdrew a bundle wrapped in material that shifted color when he tried to focus on it.

"Your sister's effects. Recovered from unit 44. Where the fire started."

"I don't have a sister."

"You had one." She held out the bundle. "She pulled you down. Kept the smoke from your lungs. The records show you died alone, which is how you survived—no witness to contradict."

His hands found the package. It weighed nothing, but his arms bent under it as if carrying something that had learned gravity's full lesson.

Through the window—he hadn't seen a window—the city showed its real face. Not vertical, but flattened, scorched, the Ossuary repeated in every direction like reflections between mirrors, each copy holding a different floor, a different moment of burning.

"Why me?"

"Because when the helmet whispers *don't trust*, you trust anyway." She touched his gloved hand where the stylus had confirmed his younger face. "The Dispatcher isn't routing you to the dead. It's routing the dead to you. You're the living archive. The one who stayed."

His roster chimed. Completion tone, singing in harmony that broke into words: *Return to origin. New load assigned.*

She was already fading into the chairs, into patient stillness.

The descent took longer than physics allowed. When the doors opened, his bike waited in the lobby, nav glowing with an address he knew without checking.

Toll's unit. 7B.

Kavi gripped the bundle and kicked off. But twenty meters down the ramp, his helmet cracked with static—not the Dispatcher's calm frequency, not the whispered warnings, but something raw and multiplying underneath.

Then Toll's voice, live and breaking: "It's happening to me too. The same six. I'm at four and she's already—"

Silence. Then the Dispatcher, somehow smiling: *Rider Harrison disconnected. Proceed without assistance.*

Kavi stopped. The bike shuddered beneath him, caught between two commands. Ahead: Toll's unit, where the fifth address waited. Behind: the passage to the Ossuary, where Lira's bundle grew warm against his ribs, where the dead had finally found their courier.

He had not chosen before. The helmet had chosen. The pattern had chosen.

Now the road split, and only his hands on the bars would decide.

He looked down. The bundle had unwrapped itself. Inside: a child's hand, small, preserved in resin or memory or both, still clutching the stylus he must have dropped ten years ago when someone pulled him down, when someone covered his mouth, when the records forgot to record that he had ever been there at all.

His hand moved. The bike turned.

## Episode 5

The bike carried him toward Toll's unit, but the road refused to shorten. Kavi rode for kilometers that should have been meters, the expressway pylons repeating like the Y in Elena's walls, each one marked with that same red character: avoid. He stopped counting at forty-seven.

Toll's 7B was dark when he arrived. No burgundy numbers, no door at all—only a rectangle of shadow where the unit had been. Kavi's helmet displayed a completion checkmark. *Delivered.* To whom, the system did not specify.

He kicked the stand down. The bundle against his ribs had cooled, but the hand inside—he could feel its fingers through the wrapping, five distinct pressures, still gripping.

Static harvested his attention. Not the Dispatcher. Something beneath it, speaking in gaps between words.

"...structural failure..."

"...accelerant pattern consistent with..."

"...no survivors recovered from 44..."

Fragments. News reports from ten years ago, compressed into his ear like dirt packed into a wound. Kavi tore the helmet off.

Silence expanded. Too much of it. The city had never been this quiet—no ventilation hum, no distant drone traffic, no rain on resin. He could hear his own blood, the particular percussion of sitting upright on a bike that wouldn't move without a command he hadn't given.

The bundle twitched.

Kavi unwrapped it by streetlight that hadn't been on when he arrived, light the color of insufficient fire. The resin preserved more than bone: a sleeve, blue plaid, scorched at the cuff but recognizable. He'd worn this. He'd worn this and dropped the stylus and someone had picked it up, someone small, someone who'd died holding it.

A chip fell out. Music data, unlabeled. He found the port in his helmet's base—he didn't know why he knew it was there—and pressed it in.

The first note was breath. The second was a child's voice, off-key, singing words he hadn't heard in ten years because no one had sung them to him, because he'd never had a sister, because the records showed him alone.

*Sleep now, kite-string heart...*

Lira's voice. Lira's lullaby. The backup vocals were smoke alarms, distant and ignored.

Kavi's hands found the throttle without his instruction. The bike woke, nav displaying an address that wasn't on any roster he'd seen.

Unit 44. The Ossuary.

"Who started the fire?" he asked the helmet.

TheDispatcher answered, smiling: *Delivery valid. Proceed.*

But underneath, woven in static's grammar, another voice: "He was there before it burned. He wanted the building empty."

Kavi gripped the bars. "Who?"

"Ask the one who delivers to him now."

The road unspooled behind him, the city stripping itself to frame and beam. Kavi rode toward the Ossuary's footprint, the bundle warm again, Lira's voice fraying at the edges like old film.

He understood something then. The deliveries weren't random. They were evidence routed to the only courier who could carry them without forgetting—because he'd already forgotten everything that mattered, and the dead needed someone empty enough to fill.

The Ossuary rose ahead, not destroyed but destroying, each window showing a different moment of July 14, 2037. In one, flames. In another, evacuation. In a third: a man in a rider's jacket, setting down a package at 44's door, walking away before the match touched the accelerant trail.

Kavi recognized the jacket. He'd thrown it out after his third route, after the stains wouldn't wash, after the smell of smoke followed him home.

The bike stopped. The hand released the stylus, and Kavi's adult fingers closed around it, understanding at last what signature the pattern needed.

Not his name. His acknowledgement.

He looked up at unit 44. Someone stood in the window. Not Lira. Older. Male. Wearing the face from the burning photograph, the one who delivered before the fire.

The helmet spoke in Toll's voice, breaking: "Don't sign for him. Please."

Kavi opened his mouth to answer, to refuse, to become something other than what the dead needed him to be—but the words that came out weren't his, weren't chosen, were spoken already in the voice of someone who had signed long before he knew what he was agreeing to: "I'm the archive. That's what we do."

## Episode 6

The stylus residue on Kavi's palm stained his lifeline gray, a print that wouldn't wash. The figure in 44's window had dissolved into the multiple-choice destruction of the facade—flames in one pane, smoke in the next, evacuation in a third, each refusing to settle into single memory.

His bike rejected the throttle. The engine caught, died, caught again in a rhythm he knew from his body: held, held, released. Someone had taught him to breathe this way. Someone small.

He left the helmet on the street. Without its buffer, the city showed its stitching—seams where buildings repeated, rust stains cloned across three consecutive walls, flood marks stopping at identical heights. The dead had built this from what they remembered.

The road to Toll's unit had closed. In its place: a narrow residential street, the kind the Ossuary had replaced decades before it burned. Numbers on doors: 42, 43, then a gap where 44 should be, then 45 with a green-oxidized knocker.

43 opened before he touched it.

A woman stood there, thirtyish, wool coat cut clean, holding a cup still steaming—as if she'd waited rather than been surprised. "You're Kavi. You don't move like the others."

"What others?"

"Riders. They stop at the gap, mark something, leave packages in the street." She stepped aside. "I'm Mira. You're here about Lira."

The name struck where the bundle rested against his chest. Inside, the walls held dozens of photographs, arranged in deliberate narrative: a foster home, two girls on a stoop. The older one sharp-eyed, arm around younger Mira in a posture he recognized from burning hallways—coverage, survival arranged for two.

"Lira died in the fire," Mira said. "I've said it until it became true. But she died saving someone, and that someone wasn't me." She found a specific photograph. "Recognition isn't memory. I know you don't remember."

Three children: young Mira, unguarded; Lira, already wearing the expression she'd die with; between them, a boy with Kavi's chin, hand in Lira's, eyes on something burning beyond the frame.

"She talked about you. Not by name. 'The one I pulled down.'" Mira set the photograph down edges-first, a rider's gesture. "Two weeks ago, deliveries started. Not food. Grief. Packages to dead names, to children who never grew. You're the sixth rider. The others signed. They forgot by morning."

Kavi's hand found empty space where the stylus had been. "Why me last?"

"Because you're the only one she actually saved." Mira's cup had cooled in her grip. "The others are carriers. You're the archive. She needs you to remember for her, now that she's finally—"

The lights flickered. For a moment he saw two rooms: this clean apartment, and its charred twin, Mira's body on the floor where flame had reached before rescue, another death the Ossuary had collected and the city had forgotten.

"She's fading," Mira said, voice rough with rehearsed acceptance. "The dead don't vanish. They distribute. She's been scattering into riders, into routes, into anything that moves. You were supposed to carry her forward, not bring her back."

Kavi looked at the photograph. The boy's hand in Lira's. The stylus he dropped, she kept, he carried until it turned to ash.

"Stop signing," Mira said. "Let her go where she's going. The archive closes when the last witness refuses."

He heard his old answer arriving, the voice that spoke before he chose: *I can't refuse what I've already agreed to.*

But when he opened his mouth, the words came broken, his own at last: "I'm not refusing. I'm choosing. Tell me where she's going. I'll ride there anyway."

Mira's cup fell. Not dropped—released. "You can't. No rider's ever—"

"I know," Kavi said. "That's why I have to."

## Episode 7

The expressway dissolved in stages—guardrails peeling like burned skin, revealing 1970s brick underneath. Rain returned, but backward, droplets rising from pavement to cloud in stuttering reverse. Kavi kept his eyes forward. The bike knew this route better than he did.

He found the building in a gap between addresses: renovated facade facing the street, scorched husk visible only in periphery. Unit 44bis, Mira had called it. The Ossuary's echo.

The door opened on a child.

She stood at adult height, wearing Lira's face but wrong-aged—twelve, the number came to him—holding a package wrapped in shifting material.

"You're late," she said. "I've been handing these off for hours."

Inside: corridors that turned back on themselves, windows showing July 14 from multiple angles, forever burning. Three riders sat at a table made from a door marked 44, helmets beside them like shed skins. They didn't look up.

"The archive's been splitting me into carriers," the child-Lira said. "Riders sign, I ride with them, they forget." She set down the package. A music chip, unmarked. "You're the last. After you, no more me to distribute."

"Then what happens?"

"Gone properly. Different from dead." She found his eyes—clearer than the woman in 9, trauma not yet accumulated. "The others didn't ask. You're asking."

"Because I'm choosing."

"Because you're the one I actually saved." She smiled, and he saw Mira's grief, and recognition between children who'd shared breath in burning darkness. "You want to carry me forward. But I'm not cargo, Kavi. I was never cargo."

She pressed the chip into his hand. It warmed immediately.

"Play it when you need to remember why you forgot."

His helmet spoke from the table, the Dispatcher's voice smiling through hollow shells: *Rider Rao. Return to origin.*

Kavi walked to the table, but reached past his own helmet—for Toll's, burgundy 7B cracked across the visor. Inside, a message breaking through static: *"It's happening to me too. The same six. I'm at four and she's already—"*

He pressed the chip into Toll's helmet.

The riders looked up together, four faces suddenly present. The child-Lira began to fade—not vanishing, but concentrating, everything distributed drawing back toward this single point.

"What did you do?"

"Shared it," Kavi said. "That's what archives do too."

Static rose like weather. Through it, Toll's live voice, finally finding frequency: *"Rao? I can see it now. The pattern. It's not six addresses. It's six of us. Riders who were there. Who—"*

Silence. Then, using Toll's broken frequency, words with weight beyond their sound:

*"Meet me where the expressway fractures. Unit 7B. Before it burns again."*

Kavi looked at the child now nearly gone, at the riders remembering their empty hands, at the window showing evacuation and flame and something new—a figure in a jacket he'd discarded, walking away from a door he'd never approached.

He moved toward the door that led out and also in, toward the fracture where aid might exist if he reached it before the fire remembered its own beginning. The other riders stirred, gathering their helmets, understanding something they couldn't yet name.

At the threshold, the child-Lira's voice found him one last time, stripped of distribution, only itself: "You're not going to meet him. You're going to save him."

"How do you know?"

"Because you still put your hand in mine when I asked," she said. "Ten years ago. You're going to do it again."

## Episode 8

Kavi's bike slowed at the junction his map displayed as empty asphalt. The repair ramps rose on either side, rusted guardrails bent like fingers in a failed grip, leading to space that shouldn't hold structure. Unit 7B crouched there, a container of shadow with Toll's burgundy numerals peeling.

He left the engine running. The door stood open.

Inside, the unit's dimensions refused consistency. What read as single-room studio expanded into corridors Kavi recognized—Ossuary hallways, the scorched kind, bleeding through walls that should have been cheap paneling. Mira's apartment had shown its twin. This place showed everything at once.

Toll sat on a folding chair in the center, helmet on his knees, visor cracked further than Kavi remembered. Blood had dried in twin tracks from his ears, old enough to flake, fresh enough to glisten at the source.

"You're early," Toll said. "I thought you'd finish the six."

"Rerouted." Kavi kept to the doorway's threshold, where the Ossuary's intrusion seemed weakest. "You said it's happening to you too."

"Same six addresses. Same dead names. I delivered to a woman in 9B who'd been ash since before I started riding." Toll's hands found the helmet's edges, pressed. "But I didn't save anyone, Rao. I wasn't there. I grew up in the eastern grid, nowhere near the Ossuary. I checked my records. I checked until the Dispatcher asked why I was checking."

"And?"

"Gaps. Three weeks in '37 with no telemetry, no deliveries, no sleep recordings." He laughed, and it came out segmented, packet-loss in human voice. "I think I was there. I think I was there and I think I forgot, and I think the forgetting is what keeps us riding."

Kavi stepped inside. The floor held firm, but the air shifted—oxygen-thin, smoke-thick, alternating without transition. "Lira said there were six of us. Riders who were there."

"She told you that?" Toll's head came up, and his eyes held the particular vacancy of someone speaking across frequencies. "She hasn't spoken to me. Only through deliveries. Packages that open to memories I shouldn't have. A classroom I never attended. A hand on my shoulder during evacuation I never made."

A sound from the corridor Toll faced—not behind him, not accessible, but present nonetheless. Footsteps. Weight carried by someone unsuited to its heft.

"The fracture's widening," Toll said. "I can feel it in my teeth. She's coming through more direct now. Not distributed. Concentrated. Because of what you did with the chip."

Kavi moved to Toll's side, the folding chair's metal cold through his gloves. "We need to move. The Ossuary footprint—"

"Is here. Is everywhere we go now." Toll stood, and his balance failed, and Kavi caught him—adult weight and something lighter beneath it, the particular heft of someone he'd carried before memory formed enough to hold. "Rao. Listen. The Dispatcher isn't suppressing our comms. It's protecting what's left of them. Every time we speak clearly, something notices. Something wants the conversation."

The footsteps resolved. A shape in the corridor's not-quite-distance: small, holding something, face turned away in the gesture of checking for followers.

"She's not a ghost," Toll whispered. "That's what I've been trying to tell you. They're not dead. They're just waiting in the only space that remembers them, and they're getting tired of waiting alone."

Kavi felt his own weight doubling—not his body, but his context, every signature binding him to their persistence. The archive wasn't a role. It was gravity.

"Lira," he called toward the shape.

It turned. Not Lira's face. His own, younger, twelve years old, eyes reflecting fire that hadn't started in this remembered hallway.

The child opened his mouth, and Kavi heard Lira's voice from his own throat, speaking words he hadn't chosen but recognized as true: "You have to let me go now. The waiting hurts too much."

"Then stop waiting," Kavi said, and the voice was his this time, broken but deliberate. "I'm here. I finally came back."

## Episode 9

The child with Kavi's face dissolved into static—not a fade, but a broadcast dispersing through corridor walls until only smoke remained, and the smoke itself was memory. Kavi's hands ached where they held Toll upright, the pressure leaving prints he knew would bruise.

"The fracture's closing," Toll whispered, his breath metallic and sweet. "She can't sustain direct contact. That's why the distribution, the riders, the forgetting—"

Kavi walked him toward the door, negotiating with a floor that insisted on multiple elevations. "How long since you slept?"

"Don't know. The unit doesn't keep time. My device always reads July 14, 2037." Toll laughed, containing frequencies outside human hearing, making Kavi's fillings ache. "I wake to packages I don't remember accepting. Contents dated tomorrow, last year. I've delivered three without looking inside. Couldn't make myself."

They reached the threshold. Outside, the bike still ran, exhaust curling upward, drawn toward the unit's roof. The repair ramps had shifted—one spiraled into fog, the other descended into absolute dark.

"She told you there were six," Toll said, gripping the frame. "I know two others. Nova, western grid, at five addresses and forgetting her childhood. Someone in the deep tunnels, no name, frequency 440.9 MHz. They broadcast when the Ossuary burns."

The helmet spoke from the handlebar: *Rider Rao. Return to origin for mandatory rest cycle.*

The voice smiled through static, something beneath it—listening, waiting.

"Don't answer," Toll said. "It marks you. Every confirmation—"

"I'm with Rider Harrison," Kavi said anyway. "Discussing route optimization."

Silence. Then through the air itself: *Rider Harrison is disconnected. Please verify his status.*

Toll's hand found Kavi's wrist, pressure precise. "Say unresponsive. Marked for collection. It's the only language it understands."

"Found Rider Harrison unresponsive. Marked for collection. Requesting solo continuation."

*Acknowledged. Proceed with caution. The Ossuary footprint extends beyond cartographic limits.*

The listening remained—pressure against Kavi's eardrums, attention preparing to return.

Toll sagged. "It protects us by lying. The dead want witness, the living want denial, and the Dispatcher—" he touched his ear, the dried blood, "—wants both. Wants us carrying messages between states."

## Episode 18

Kavi rode with Toll's blood on his sleeve, the stain mapping familiar topography—July's heat on fabric, the geometry of old wounds reopening. The depot fell away behind them, spiral of bicycles still humming coordinates through concrete memory, but ahead the city unspooled wrong. Buildings softened at edges. Streetlights flickered in sequences that spelled no address he knew, yet his hands turned the bars toward each one.

"The Ossuary's foundation," Toll said. His voice came through the open air now, helmet abandoned, but it carried the layered quality Kavi had learned to fear—one throat, multiple origins. "They paved it. Parking structure. Level minus seven sealed in concrete and official memory."

Kavi said nothing. The red package hung flat against his chest, emptied of Mira's pulse, yet his ribs still moved with borrowed rhythm—three heartbeats, four, the arithmetic of survival he couldn't perform alone.

Toll's bike drifted close, handlebars almost touching. "My ears are ringing. Not tinnitus. The frequency. 440.9. She's on every channel now."

"Lira?"

"All of them." Toll's grin showed teeth he hadn't had in previous hours, or hadn't noticed. "The archived, the distributed, the ones who woke in warehouses with July erased. They're singing her back. Level Seven isn't where we go. It's where they come through."

The street ended. Kavi hadn't braked. The Ossuary's footprint spread before them, concrete slab over absence, parking lines painted in geometries that described no vehicle's needs. His tires crossed white paint and the surface changed—resonance, density, memory of structure beneath. The bike's suspension responded to floors that no longer existed.

Toll dismounted first. Blood traced fresh paths, not from ears now but corners of his eyes, thin and deliberate as cartography. "Integration," he said, touching the wetness, examining it with curious detachment. "They're finishing the transfer. I've been riding for ten years not knowing I was already part of the infrastructure."

Kavi killed his engine. The silence that followed was incomplete—concrete breathing, rebar remembering load, the specific frequency of spaces that had contained screaming. He found the sealed access panel by touch, fingers reading welded seams where a stairwell had been.

"Four witnesses," he said. "Elena refused privately. Mira refused publicly. You—"

"Refused to notice." Toll pressed his palm to the weld, blood smearing in patterns that might have been intentional. "Rode the same morning every July, delivered to the same dead addresses, thought it was burnout, thought it was routine, thought the city was simply full of ghosts because cities are." He laughed, wet, intimate. "I was the infrastructure's refusal to acknowledge itself. Useful. Renewable. Never questioning why my account never showed downtime."

The weld warmed under his hand. Not imagination. Thermal memory, ten years compressed into conductive moment.

"And you?" Toll turned. The blood from his eyes had reached his jaw, twin tracks converging. "You woke in a warehouse with no before. What did you refuse, Kavi?"

The panel groaned. Seals releasing, not mechanically—the concrete's own decision, or the dead's accumulated pressure. Beyond it, darkness absolute and absolute familiarity. Kavi had descended these stairs before. In dreams. In the moments between tracks on music chips. In the spaces where Lira's hand had pulled him not toward safety but toward *bearing witness*.

He stepped through.

Toll followed, their boots synchronized on steps that counted backward, thirteen becoming twelve becoming the specific number that described his sister's age when she calculated sufficient air for one descent.

"Tell me," Toll whispered, and his voice came from above now, and below, and the sealed space between, "tell me you can hear them too. The ones who didn't refuse. The ones who let the network edit them into something that could keep riding."

Kavi heard. Not voices. The shape of waiting. Forty-four presences arranged in the dark, patient as infrastructure, beautiful as Toll had promised, each occupying the exact space where remembering had become impossible.

And closest, breathing with his own childhood cadence, someone who shared his face and his wound and his refusal to become administrative:

"You're not the fourth witness," his own voice said, emerging from darkness with administrative warmth. "You're the gap they needed. The permission. Welcome to Level Seven, archive. We've been waiting to remember through you."
